What this is

A two-player turn-based tactics game on a grid, in the Fire Emblem mould. Built by Ezra, Remea and Geol, with the rules layer written here. This page is both a live test of those rules and the record of why they work the way they do.

How the code is arranged

Three parts, and the split matters more than anything else in the project:

PartOwnsKnows about
shared/The rulesNothing. No DOM, no network.
client/Drawing and inputImports the rules.
server/Sockets and roomsImports the same rules.
WhyThe client draws the combat forecast before you attack; the server decides what actually happens. If those were two implementations of the same maths, they would drift, and the panel would promise 7 damage while the server dealt 6. One file, both consumers, so they cannot disagree.

It also means the rules are testable with no browser and no server. A whole battle can be simulated in milliseconds.

One repository, not two

The client deploys to Vercel and the server will run on a VPS, so splitting the repo is tempting. Don't. With one repo, adding a field to the game state is one commit that changes both halves together. With two, it is two commits that must land together, and when they drift you get a client sending something the server does not understand — which looks like a bug rather than a version mismatch.

Movement

A budget, not a pattern

Chess pieces move in shapes: rooks on lines, knights in an L. This game does not. Each unit has a number of movement points, each tile costs points to enter, and a unit may take any route it can afford, turning as often as it likes.

Why it mattersIt is the reason terrain can be interesting. A movement pattern cannot express "the forest is slow"; a budget can.

Dijkstra, not breadth-first search

The reachable set is computed with Dijkstra's algorithm. The obvious choice is a breadth-first flood fill, and it is wrong here.

WhyBFS finds the route with the fewest steps. Once a forest costs 2 and a road costs 1, fewest steps is not cheapest. BFS produces overlays that look almost right, which is worse than obviously wrong.

Four directions, no diagonals

Units move up, down, left and right only.

WhyIf a diagonal step costs the same as an orthogonal one, moving diagonally is strictly cheaper than two orthogonal steps. Everyone zigzags, and chokepoints stop working. Four-way also makes attack range plain Manhattan distance, which a player can count by eye. Into the Breach reached the same conclusion from the other direction: its postmortem says diagonal attacks were cut because they could not be drawn legibly on a grid.

Terrain costs differ per movement type

TerrainFootHorseDefence

WhyThis exists because of a bug we found by running the numbers. Cavalry had more health, more attack, the same defence, more speed and more movement than infantry — strictly better at everything, so infantry had no reason to exist. The genre's answer is not to nerf the stats, it is to make the map do the balancing. Cavalry owns open ground and pays for rough terrain. Infantry goes where horses cannot.

Blocking rules

  • You may move through allies, but never stop on one.
  • Enemies block movement entirely.
WhyThe second rule is what lets two units stand shoulder to shoulder and hold a bridge. It is the most satisfying thing in the genre and it falls out of one line.

Combat

damage = max(1, attacker.atk - defender.def)
hit    = clamp(attacker.hit - defender.avo, 0, 100)
doubles if attacker.spd - defender.spd >= 4

Then three steps in order: the attacker strikes, the defender counters if it is alive and the attacker is within its range, and whoever is four or more speed faster strikes again.

Why the counter rule mattersIt is what makes range meaningful. An archer at distance 2 hitting a swordsman takes no counter at all. That single asymmetry generates most of the early tactical depth for one line of code.

The dice lie, on purpose

A displayed hit chance is not the real one. The game rolls twice and averages before comparing:

ShownActual
90%98.1%
70%82.3%
50%50.5%
30%18.3%
10%2.1%
WhyPeople are poor at intuiting probability. Players read 90% as "basically certain" and feel cheated when it misses. Fire Emblem has used this two-roll average since 2002 for exactly that reason: high numbers become reliable, low numbers become long shots, and the displayed figure behaves the way players already assume it does.

Cavalry beats infantry, infantry beats archer, archer beats cavalry. The last row looks broken but is the correct half of the loop: archers should not beat armour.

Why we knowNone of this was designed on paper. The numbers were run, the first pass gave archers 1 damage against infantry (sixteen rounds to kill), the fix made cavalry dominant, and the triangle fell out of correcting that. Runnable rules before a user interface is the whole reason it was caught in an evening.

Colour

Factions and overlays cannot share hues

Nintendo's convention since 2003 is blue for movement, red for attack, green for staves. Advance Wars deliberately breaks it and uses green for movement instead.

WhyBecause Advance Wars colour-codes its armies, so it cannot spend blue and red on overlays too. Ours are blue and red as well. Pick the faction palette first, then give the overlays what is left.

The palette belongs to the viewer, not the game

Palette is a browser preference. It is never sent over the wire, so two players can view the same match differently. More importantly, the slots are semantic — friendly and hostile — and each client fills them based on which side it is on.

WhyIn Fire Emblem you are always blue and the enemy always red. Pin blue to player 1 and player 2 spends the entire game with every instinct inverted.

Colour is never the only channel

Every overlay carries a pattern as well as a hue: solid, hatched, cross-hatched, dotted. Toggle "Patterns" off above to see how much is lost.

WhyRoughly 8% of men have a colour vision deficiency. Fire Emblem players have been asking for a colourblind mode since the GameBoy Advance era and no entry has ever shipped one. Two extra palettes and a pattern channel cost an afternoon.

Threat, and the question nobody answers

Two overlays, deliberately different:

  • Attack range — what the selected unit could hit, from anywhere it can reach.
  • Threat — every tile the enemy could reach and attack.

Then a third mode that almost no game has. "If I moved away" recomputes the threat with your own units erased from the board.

"When I move a unit out of an enemy's way their danger zone grows larger and can suddenly attack a previously safe unit. Is there a way to make the game assume all my units are already out of the way?"
WhyThat is the single most common complaint about danger overlays, and no Fire Emblem answers it. Battle for Wesnoth is the only game found that does, on Ctrl+B. The standard overlay tells you where you are safe; this one tells you where you are safe after you move, which is the decision you are actually making. It costs one line, because the threat function already takes the unit list: 
threatRange(0, grid, units.filter(u => u.owner !== 0))

The overlay also deliberately over-reports: tiles occupied by an enemy's own allies still count as places they could attack from. Fire Emblem 8 excludes them and lt-maker does not.

WhyUnder-reporting a threat is the failure that loses players. Over-reporting merely makes them cautious.

Turns

Phase-based: move all of your units, then hand over. Each unit tracks hasMoved and hasActed separately — a unit may move then act, or move then wait, but never act then move.

Why phasesYou can bait with one unit and follow up with another in the same phase. Unit-by-unit alternation destroys that, and combined manoeuvre is the point of the genre.

A move is provisional until a command is chosen. Cancel before that and the unit returns to where it started. Choosing any command, including Wait, is the commit. Advance Wars: Days of Ruin puts it best — "until orders have been finalized, you can back out of menus."

WhyThe weight of a tactics game should sit on outcomes, not on input mistakes. A misclick should cost nothing; a dead unit should cost everything.
